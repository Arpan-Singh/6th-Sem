Q1. What is PERT? Explain the steps with a numerical example. Differentiate PERT and CPM.Most asked — appears almost every year
⌄
Introduction: PERT (Programme Evaluation and Review Technique) is a network analysis tool for planning and controlling projects with uncertain activity durations, developed by the U.S. Navy in 1958 for the Polaris missile project.

Three Time Estimates

  • Optimistic Time (to): Minimum time if everything goes perfectly.
  • Most Likely Time (tm): Normal realistic estimate.
  • Pessimistic Time (tp): Maximum time if things go wrong.
Expected Time (te) = (to + 4tm + tp) / 6    |    Variance (σ²) = [(tp − to) / 6]²

Steps in PERT

  1. Identify all project activities.
  2. Determine sequence and precedence relationships.
  3. Draw network diagram (nodes = events, arrows = activities).
  4. Estimate to, tm, tp for each activity; calculate te.
  5. Forward pass → Calculate Earliest Start (ES) and Earliest Finish (EF).
  6. Backward pass → Calculate Latest Start (LS) and Latest Finish (LF).
  7. Calculate Float = LS − ES for each activity.
  8. Identify Critical Path (all activities with Float = 0).

Numerical Example

Activitytotmtpte = (to+4tm+tp)/6
A (1→2)2464
B (1→3)3575
C (2→4)4686
D (3→4)1353
E (4→5)2464

Path 1→2→4→5 = 4+6+4 = 14 weeks  |  Path 1→3→4→5 = 5+3+4 = 12 weeks

Critical Path = 1→2→4→5 (14 weeks)

PERT vs CPM

BasisPERTCPM
NatureProbabilisticDeterministic
Time estimatesThree (to, tm, tp)One (single estimate)
FocusTime managementTime-cost trade-off
Best forR&D, new projectsConstruction, repetitive work
Cost crashingNot applicableCost crashing done
Conclusion: PERT suits uncertain/research projects while CPM suits well-defined construction projects. Both enable managers to plan, schedule and control complex projects effectively.
VERY HIGH15 MarksUnit I
Q2. Explain the Project Life Cycle and its phases. Discuss Generation and Screening of Project Ideas.Guaranteed 1 question from this topic
⌄
Introduction: A project is a temporary endeavor to create a unique product or service. The Project Life Cycle is the series of stages a project passes through from initiation to closure.

Four Phases of Project Life Cycle

  1. Initiation Phase: Project idea born, feasibility assessed, project charter created. Key question: "Is the project worth doing?"
  2. Planning Phase: Detailed scope, schedule, budget, and resource planning. Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) prepared. Risk identification done.
  3. Execution Phase: Actual work performed. Resources deployed. Project manager coordinates team. Progress monitored against plan.
  4. Monitoring & Control Phase: Ongoing throughout execution. Track KPIs, manage changes, resolve issues.
  5. Closure Phase: Project formally completed. Deliverables handed over. Post-implementation review done. Lessons learned documented.

Generation of Project Ideas

  • Analysis of performance gaps in existing industries
  • Study of imports to identify import-substitution opportunities
  • Technology developments creating new product possibilities
  • Government policy announcements and incentive schemes
  • Consumer survey and market research findings
  • Suggestions from R&D departments and trade journals
  • Visiting trade fairs, exhibitions, and international markets

Screening of Project Ideas (Preliminary Screening)

After generating ideas, they are screened to select the most promising ones for detailed feasibility study. Screening criteria include:

  • Compatibility: Aligns with promoter's competence and goals.
  • Viability: Technically and commercially feasible.
  • Resource Availability: Raw materials, finance, manpower available.
  • Market Potential: Adequate demand exists or can be created.
  • Government Policy: No licensing restrictions; incentives available.
  • Profitability: Reasonable returns expected.
Memory Trick: Project Life Cycle = IPEMC: Initiation → Planning → Execution → Monitoring → Closure
Conclusion: The project life cycle provides a structured framework for managing projects. Systematic idea generation and rigorous screening ensure that only viable projects proceed to the resource-intensive feasibility and implementation stages.

Q3. Discuss the various sources of financing a project. Differentiate between debt and equity financing.Unit III always gets 1 long question
⌄
Introduction: Project financing refers to mobilizing funds for investment in a project. The choice of financing mix (capital structure) critically affects the project's viability, risk, and returns.

A. Long-Term Sources of Finance

  1. Equity Shares: Ownership capital. No fixed obligation. Shareholders bear highest risk but get residual profits (dividends).
  2. Preference Shares: Hybrid — fixed dividend like debt, part ownership. Priority over equity in dividend and winding-up.
  3. Debentures/Bonds: Debt instrument. Fixed interest obligation. No ownership dilution. Types: secured, unsecured, convertible.
  4. Term Loans from Banks/FIs: Long-term loans from commercial banks, IDBI, IFCI, SIDBI. Most common source for Indian projects.
  5. Retained Earnings: Internal source. No cost of raising. Used for expansion by profitable companies.

B. Short-Term / Working Capital Sources

  • Bank overdraft and cash credit
  • Trade credit from suppliers
  • Commercial paper and factoring

C. Special Sources

  • Venture Capital: Private equity investment in early-stage high-growth startups. VC takes equity stake and provides mentorship. Example: Sequoia, Nexus Venture Partners.
  • Angel Investors: High-net-worth individuals investing in startups at seed stage.
  • Government Grants & Subsidies: For backward area development, SSI, green energy.
  • Foreign Direct Investment (FDI): Foreign funds through automatic route or government approval route.
  • ECB (External Commercial Borrowings): Foreign currency loans from international markets.

Debt vs Equity Financing

HIGH15 MarksUnit IV
Q4. What is Social Cost-Benefit Analysis (SCBA)? How does it differ from commercial analysis?Asked almost every year in Section B
⌄
Introduction: Social Cost-Benefit Analysis (SCBA) evaluates a project from society's perspective, not just the investor's. It considers all impacts — economic, social, and environmental — including externalities not captured by market prices.

Key Concepts in SCBA

  • Shadow Prices: Market prices adjusted to reflect true social value. Foreign exchange overvaluation corrected using shadow exchange rate.
  • Social Discount Rate: Rate used to discount future social benefits/costs. Usually lower than commercial rate, reflecting society's lower time preference.
  • Externalities: Positive (job creation, education) are social benefits; Negative (pollution, displacement) are social costs.
  • Employment Effects: Labor-intensive projects valued higher in surplus labor economies like India.
  • Distribution Effects: Projects benefiting poor/marginalized communities get higher social weight.

SCBA vs Commercial Analysis

BasisCommercial AnalysisSCBA
PerspectiveFirm/investorSociety as a whole
PricesMarket pricesShadow/accounting prices
Discount rateMarket/commercial rateSocial discount rate
ExternalitiesIgnoredExplicitly included
EmploymentJust a costBenefit (social value)
GoalProfit maximizationSocial welfare maximization

Applications of SCBA

  • Infrastructure projects — dams, highways, power plants
  • Public health and education projects
  • World Bank and ADB-funded projects (mandatory SCBA)
  • Environmental conservation and urban development
Conclusion: SCBA bridges private profitability and social welfare. A project may be commercially unviable yet socially desirable (e.g., rural electrification). Governments use SCBA to ensure public resources maximize overall social welfare.

Q5. What is Technical Analysis in project management? Explain Market Analysis and its components.Unit II theory portion — frequently asked
⌄

Technical Analysis

Technical analysis evaluates whether the project is technically feasible. It examines:

  • Technology Selection: Which technology to use? Import or develop locally? Proven vs new technology.
  • Location Analysis: Proximity to raw materials, markets, labor, infrastructure, and government incentives.
  • Plant Capacity: Optimum scale of production — economies of scale vs capital investment trade-off.
  • Raw Material Availability: Quality, quantity, price, seasonal availability.
  • Infrastructure: Power, water, transport, communication requirements.
  • Environmental Clearances: Pollution norms, EIA (Environmental Impact Assessment).

Market Analysis

Market analysis determines the demand potential for the project's product/service. Steps:

  1. Market Survey: Primary (questionnaire, interview) and secondary (government reports, industry data) research.
  2. Demand Estimation: Current demand and historical growth rate analysis.
  3. Demand Forecasting: Future demand projection using trend analysis, regression, or econometric models.
  4. Market Characterization: Geographic spread, customer segments, buying behavior.
  5. Market Planning: Pricing strategy, distribution channels, promotional plans.
  6. Competitive Analysis: Existing players, their capacity, market share, strengths/weaknesses.
Conclusion: Technical and market analysis are cornerstones of project feasibility. A technically sound project with weak market demand will fail; a market opportunity without technical feasibility cannot be exploited. Both analyses together determine project viability.
MODERATE15 MarksUnit IV
Q6. Explain Project Monitoring and Control. What are the emerging concepts in Project Management?Unit IV theory — often Section B option
⌄

Project Monitoring and Control

Project monitoring is the systematic tracking of project progress against planned targets. Control involves taking corrective action when deviations occur.

  • Tools: Gantt charts, S-Curves, Milestone tracking, Earned Value Management (EVM).
  • Earned Value Management (EVM): Measures project performance by integrating scope, schedule, and cost. Key metrics: SPI (Schedule Performance Index), CPI (Cost Performance Index).
  • Variance Analysis: Cost variance (CV) = Earned Value − Actual Cost. Schedule variance (SV) = Earned Value − Planned Value.
  • Post-Project Evaluation (Post Audit): Compares actual outcomes with planned. Identifies lessons learned.
  • Abandonment Analysis: When to terminate a failing project — sunk cost fallacy must be ignored; only future costs/benefits matter.

Emerging Concepts in Project Management

  • Agile Project Management: Iterative, flexible approach for software and innovation projects. Works in sprints (2–4 weeks). Key frameworks: Scrum, Kanban.
  • Risk Management: Systematic identification, analysis, and mitigation of project risks. Risk register, Monte Carlo simulation.
  • IT in Project Management: Software tools like MS Project, Primavera, Jira, Asana, Trello enable real-time project tracking.
  • Sustainable Project Management: Incorporating environmental and social impact assessment into project decisions.
  • Virtual/Remote Project Teams: Managing geographically dispersed teams using collaboration tools.
Conclusion: Effective monitoring and control is the difference between project success and failure. Modern projects increasingly blend traditional PERT/CPM methods with Agile approaches and digital tools to manage complexity and uncertainty.
HIGH5 MarksUnit II
Q7. What is Critical Path Method (CPM)? State the key formulas and steps.Often asked as 5-mark short question
⌄
CPM (Critical Path Method) is a deterministic project management technique that identifies the longest sequence of dependent activities (the critical path) that determines the minimum project completion time.
ES (Earliest Start) = MAX of all EF of predecessors
EF (Earliest Finish) = ES + Duration
LF (Latest Finish) = MIN of all LS of successors
LS (Latest Start) = LF − Duration
Total Float = LS − ES = LF − EF
Critical Path: all activities where Total Float = 0

Steps: (1) List activities & durations → (2) Draw network → (3) Forward pass (ES, EF) → (4) Backward pass (LF, LS) → (5) Calculate Float → (6) Identify critical path.

Introduction

Human Resource Management (HRM) is the strategic, integrated, and coherent approach to the management of people within an organization, enabling them to contribute effectively to the achievement of organizational objectives. It encompasses all activities related to the acquisition, development, compensation, integration, and maintenance of human resources. Peter Drucker called people "the only asset that can appreciate in value over time."

A. Functions of the HR Manager

The functions are broadly classified as Managerial, Operative, and Advisory:

1. Managerial Functions
  • Planning: Forecasting future HR needs — how many people, what skills, when and where. Aligning the workforce plan with the organizational strategy.
  • Organizing: Structuring the HR department, defining roles, establishing reporting relationships, creating job descriptions and specifications.
  • Directing: Motivating, leading, and guiding employees toward organizational goals. Involves leadership, motivation, and communication.
  • Controlling: Setting performance standards, measuring actual performance against standards, and taking corrective action where deviations exist.
2. Operative (Core) Functions
  • Procurement: Job analysis, HR planning, recruitment, selection, placement, and induction of new employees.
  • Development: Training and development programs, career planning, management development, and performance appraisal.
  • Compensation: Job evaluation, wage and salary administration, incentives, bonus, fringe benefits, and social security measures.
  • Integration: Motivating employees, handling grievances, maintaining industrial relations, collective bargaining, and ensuring employee participation.
  • Maintenance: Ensuring safe working conditions, employee health, welfare programs, and retirement benefits.
3. Advisory Functions
  • Advising top management on formulating HR policies and HR strategy.
  • Advising line managers on discipline, grievance handling, and workforce issues.

B. HR Policies

HR Policies are pre-determined guidelines, rules, and principles that govern how an organization manages its people and guides managers in decision-making on HR matters.

Characteristics of Good HR Policies
  • Clear, written, and communicated to all employees.
  • Consistent with legal requirements and industry standards.
  • Flexible enough to adapt to changing circumstances.
  • Fair and equitable — apply uniformly across the organization.

C. Emerging Challenges in HRM

1. Workforce Diversity

Modern organizations employ people from different genders, generations, ethnicities, religions, and nationalities. Managing diversity means leveraging differences as a strength — diverse teams are more creative and innovative. HR must create inclusive policies, prevent discrimination, and build cultural competence among managers.

2. Employee Welfare

Welfare measures include both statutory (Employees' State Insurance, Provident Fund, gratuity) and voluntary benefits (crèche facilities, recreational clubs, subsidized meals, housing). Welfare improves morale, reduces absenteeism, and boosts loyalty. Companies like Infosys and TCS invest heavily in welfare infrastructure.

3. Health and Safety

The Factories Act 1948 mandates safe working conditions — adequate lighting, ventilation, sanitation, guards on machines, and first-aid facilities. Post-COVID, mental health has become equally important. HR must develop safety policies, conduct safety audits, and establish Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs) for mental health support.

4. Social Security

Social security protects employees against risks — old age (PF, Gratuity), illness (ESI, Group Mediclaim), disability, and unemployment. HR manages statutory compliance with EPFO, ESIC, and Payment of Gratuity Act to ensure legal and ethical obligations are met.

5. Employee Empowerment

Empowerment means giving employees the authority, resources, and responsibility to make decisions in their domain without seeking constant managerial approval. It increases ownership, creativity, and job satisfaction. HR enables empowerment through participative management, quality circles, and self-managed teams.

6. Downsizing

Downsizing refers to the planned reduction of the workforce to improve efficiency and reduce costs, often triggered by technological disruption, automation, or business restructuring. HR's role is to: (a) identify roles for elimination objectively, (b) design VRS and severance packages, (c) manage survivor syndrome — the psychological impact on retained employees, and (d) ensure legal compliance with retrenchment laws.

7. Voluntary Retirement Scheme (VRS)

VRS is a mechanism for organizations to reduce their workforce without forced retrenchment, by offering attractive financial packages to employees who choose to retire voluntarily. A good VRS package typically includes: lump-sum payment (often 45 days' salary per year of service), continuation of medical benefits, pension benefits. VRS is governed by Section 2(oo)(b) of the Industrial Disputes Act 1947. Famous examples: BSNL's VRS 2019, Central Government VRS schemes.

8. Work-Life Balance

The blurring of home and work boundaries — especially post-pandemic — has made work-life balance a critical HR agenda. Strategies include flexible work hours, work-from-home policies, mandatory leave policies, anti-overwork campaigns, Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs), and manager training to respect personal time boundaries. Burnout recognition and prevention is now a core HR competency.

D. Employee Code of Conduct

A Code of Conduct is a formal document outlining the ethical standards, behavioral expectations, and professional norms that every employee is expected to uphold.

  • Integrity and Honesty: No fraud, misrepresentation, or conflict of interest.
  • Confidentiality: Protection of company data, trade secrets, and client information.
  • Respect at Workplace: Zero tolerance for harassment, discrimination, and bullying. Compliance with POSH Act 2013.
  • Compliance: Following all laws, regulations, and company policies.
  • Use of Company Resources: Proper use of company assets, IT systems, and intellectual property.
  • Social Media Policy: Guidelines on representing the company on personal social media.

E. Human Resource Information System (HRIS)

HRIS is an integrated software platform that combines HR management with information technology to collect, store, process, and report HR-related data, enabling efficient HR administration and data-driven decision-making.

F. e-HRM (Electronic Human Resource Management)

e-HRM is the application of web-based technologies to HR processes — allowing employees, managers, and HR professionals to interact with HR systems anytime, anywhere through digital platforms.

Types of e-HRM
  • Operational e-HRM: Digitization of routine HR administrative tasks — payroll processing, leave applications, expense claims, document management.
  • Relational e-HRM: Technology supporting HR processes that involve employee interaction — online recruitment portals, e-learning platforms, performance management systems, employee self-service portals.
  • Transformational e-HRM: Strategic use of HR technology for high-value activities — HR analytics dashboards, AI-powered talent intelligence, succession planning tools, workforce planning models.
Benefits of e-HRM
  • Reduces administrative burden and paper-based processes.
  • Enables employee and manager self-service — faster resolution of routine queries.
  • Provides real-time HR data for decision-making.
  • Improves accuracy and compliance (automated statutory filings).
  • Scales efficiently as the organization grows.

Introduction

Staffing is the managerial function of filling and keeping filled the positions in the organizational structure. Unit II covers the complete staffing pipeline — from workforce planning to onboarding and retaining talent. These are the most operationally critical HR activities that directly determine the quality of the organization's human capital.

A. Human Resource Planning (HRP)

HRP is the process of forecasting the organization's future demand for human resources and ensuring supply matches that demand — the right people, right skills, right place, right time.

HR Planning Process (SDGAM)
  • S — Supply Analysis: Audit current workforce using HRIS data — headcount, skills inventory, age profile, retirement projections, attrition rates. Sources: internal (promotions, transfers) and external (labor market).
  • D — Demand Forecasting: Project future HR needs based on organizational plans. Quantitative methods: trend analysis, ratio analysis, regression. Qualitative: Delphi technique, managerial judgment.
  • G — Gap Analysis: Demand − Supply = Gap. Identify surplus (more people than needed) or deficit (fewer people) in each skill/role category.
  • A — Action Planning: Deficit → recruit, train, or hire contract staff. Surplus → redeploy, retrain, VRS/downsizing, freeze hiring.
  • M — Monitor & Control: Track implementation quarterly. Update forecasts as business plans change.
Dimensions of HRP
  • Quantitative: How many people are needed? Numerical headcount planning.
  • Qualitative: What skills, competencies, and roles? Ensures capability, not just numbers.

B. Job Analysis

Job Analysis is the systematic process of collecting detailed information about a job's duties, responsibilities, required skills, and working conditions — the foundation for all HR activities.

Methods of Job Analysis
  • Interview Method: HR interviews job incumbents and supervisors. Rich qualitative data but time-consuming.
  • Questionnaire Method: Structured forms (Position Analysis Questionnaire — PAQ) filled by employees. Scalable but may lack depth.
  • Observation Method: Analyst directly observes employee performing the job. Effective for manual jobs; difficult for cognitive roles.
  • Diary/Log Method: Employees record activities daily. Accurate for complex knowledge roles.
  • Critical Incidents Method: Collects specific examples of excellent and poor job performance from supervisors.

C. Recruitment

Recruitment is the process of attracting, screening, and creating a qualified pool of applicants for open positions. It is a positive process — you are inviting people in.

Internal Sources of Recruitment
  • Promotion: Moving a current employee to a higher grade/position.
  • Transfer: Lateral movement across departments or locations.
  • Employee Referral Programs (ERP): Existing employees recommend qualified candidates — typically incentivized with referral bonuses. High-quality, culturally-fit hires.
  • Re-employment / Rehiring: Rehiring former employees who left on good terms ("boomerang employees").
  • Internal Job Posting: Vacancies advertised on company intranet for internal applications.
External Sources of Recruitment
  • Online Job Portals: Naukri.com, LinkedIn, Indeed — widest reach for most roles.
  • Campus Recruitment: College placements — fresher hiring; strong pipeline for entry-level talent (IIT/IIM placements by top firms).
  • Employment Agencies: Public (National Career Service Centres) and private (TeamLease, Manpower Group).
  • Executive Search / Headhunting: For senior/specialized roles. Firms: Spencer Stuart, Korn Ferry.
  • Walk-ins and Job Fairs: Useful for volume hiring (BPO, retail).
  • Social Media Recruiting: LinkedIn Recruiter, Twitter, GitHub for tech talent.

D. Selection Process

Selection is the process of choosing the most suitable candidate from the recruitment pool — a negative/elimination process (rejecting unsuitable candidates to find the one).

Steps in the Selection Process
  1. Preliminary Screening: Review applications/CVs against minimum criteria (qualifications, experience). ATS software automates keyword matching.
  2. Selection Tests: Aptitude, intelligence, personality, trade/skill tests (discussed below).
  3. Employment Interview: Structured, semi-structured, or behavioral (STAR method) interviews. Panel, one-on-one, or video interviews.
  4. Background Verification: Check employment history, qualifications, criminal records, references. Critical for sensitive roles.
  5. Medical Examination: Ensures physical fitness for job requirements.
  6. Final Selection Decision: Hiring manager + HR make offer decision based on all data points.
  7. Job Offer Letter: Formal written offer specifying salary, grade, joining date, and terms.

Introduction

Training is a short-term, systematic process of improving employee knowledge, skills, and attitudes for performing a specific job. Development is a long-term process focused on the overall growth and future potential of the individual — preparing employees for future roles. Both are part of the Learning and Development (L&D) function in modern HR.

B. Importance of Training

  • Bridges the gap between current performance and required performance.
  • Reduces wastage, errors, accidents, and rework.
  • Increases employee motivation, confidence, and job satisfaction.
  • Enables organizations to adapt to technological change (reskilling).
  • Reduces supervision requirements as employees become self-sufficient.
  • Builds a succession pipeline — develops future managers internally.
  • Reduces attrition — employees who receive training feel valued and invested in.
  • Improves organizational adaptability and innovation capacity.

C. On-the-Job Training (OJT) Methods

OJT takes place at the actual workstation while performing the job. Learning by doing in a real work environment.

1. Apprenticeship Training

A structured, long-term program (1–4 years) where a trainee learns a trade or craft by working under the guidance of a skilled worker. Government-regulated under the Apprentices Act 1961. Common in engineering, trades, and medical fields. Example: ITI apprenticeships at manufacturing companies like Maruti, L&T.

2. Job Rotation

Systematically moving employees through different departments, functions, or roles over a planned schedule — building breadth of experience. Benefits: reduces boredom, builds cross-functional understanding, identifies hidden talents, creates versatile employees. Example: Management trainee programs at banks rotate trainees through branches, loans, treasury, and HR.

3. Coaching

An ongoing, one-on-one development relationship where a manager/senior employee regularly guides, instructs, and provides feedback to help the employee improve specific skills or overcome challenges. Unlike mentoring, coaching is job-focused and performance-linked. Coaching sessions use the GROW model (Goal, Reality, Options, Will).

4. Mentoring

A long-term developmental relationship between an experienced senior professional (mentor) and a less experienced person (mentee) — focused on career guidance, wisdom transfer, and holistic professional growth. Mentoring goes beyond job tasks to include career navigation, organizational politics, values, and personal development. Example: Google's formal mentoring programs, TCS's Senior Leader mentoring initiative.

5. Understudy Method

The trainee is designated as the backup/successor for a specific senior role and shadows that person closely — attending meetings, working on their projects, and gradually taking on their responsibilities. Specifically designed for succession planning. Example: A Deputy Manager designated as understudy for the Regional Manager — preparing for succession.

D. Off-the-Job Training Methods

Training conducted outside the actual workplace — in classrooms, training centers, or simulation environments. Allows focused, uninterrupted learning.

6. Classroom / Lecture Method

Traditional instructor-led training in a classroom setting. Efficient for delivering conceptual/theoretical knowledge to large groups simultaneously. Limitations: one-way, low interactivity, retention may be poor without reinforcement. Enhanced with multimedia, Q&A sessions, and quizzes.

7. Case Study Method

Participants analyze real or simulated business situations, diagnose problems, and propose solutions — developing analytical, decision-making, and communication skills. Harvard Business School pioneered this method for management education. Participants must apply theoretical concepts to complex, ambiguous real-world scenarios.

8. Role Playing

Participants enact roles in simulated work scenarios — practicing interpersonal skills, negotiation, conflict resolution, or customer handling in a safe environment. Highly effective for developing behavioral competencies (empathy, assertiveness, listening). Example: Customer service training where participants role-play difficult customer interactions.

9. Vestibule Training

Training conducted in a specially created replica of the actual workplace — using the same equipment and workflows but without production pressure. Common in manufacturing, aviation (flight simulators), and banking (practice banking environment). Advantage: realistic without risking actual production loss or accidents.

10. In-Basket Exercise

Participant is given a simulated manager's inbox (emails, memos, reports, problems) and must prioritize and respond in a set time — testing decision-making, time management, and managerial judgment. Widely used in Assessment Centres for management selection and development.

11. Management Games / Business Simulations

Teams compete in simulated business scenarios — making strategic, operational, and financial decisions. The simulation model shows the consequences of each team's decisions — creating powerful experiential learning. Example: Capsim, Marketplace Live simulations used in B-schools. Develops strategic thinking, teamwork, and decision-making under uncertainty.

12. e-Learning / Computer-Based Training (CBT)

Online learning delivered through Learning Management Systems (LMS) — Coursera, LinkedIn Learning, Udemy for Business, Moodle. Includes video lectures, quizzes, simulations, and gamification elements. Advantages: self-paced, scalable (1 to 100,000 learners), cost-effective, accessible 24/7 from any device, trackable completion and assessments. Example: Infosys uses iLearn LMS for mandatory compliance training across 300,000+ employees.

13. Sensitivity Training (T-Group Training)

An unstructured small-group experience where participants examine their interpersonal behavior, emotional reactions, and impact on others — developing self-awareness, empathy, and emotional intelligence. Participants give and receive candid, personal feedback in a safe facilitator-led environment. Primarily used for leadership development and creating inclusive workplace cultures.

E. Training Need Analysis (TNA)

TNA is the systematic process of identifying the gap between current and required competencies to determine where training investment is most needed and what kind of training will address it.

Three Levels of TNA
  • Organizational Level Analysis: What strategic goals require new capabilities? Are there any organizational-wide performance gaps (quality, customer satisfaction, safety)? What cultural changes require new mindsets? Data: business plan, customer complaints, quality metrics, leadership assessments.
  • Task/Operational Level Analysis: What does each job require in terms of knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs)? Use job analysis, competency frameworks, and task inventory. Identify the gap between what the job demands and what current employees can do.
  • Person/Individual Level Analysis: Which specific employees have performance gaps? Use performance appraisal data, 360-degree feedback, assessment center results, and self-assessment. Identify who needs what specific training.

F. Transfer of Training (TPO — Training, Performance, Outcomes)

Transfer of Training refers to the extent to which knowledge, skills, and behaviors learned in training are actually applied back on the job — the ultimate measure of training effectiveness.

Reasons Why Transfer May Not Happen
  • Manager does not support applying new skills — "we don't do it that way here."
  • No opportunity to practice new skills in the actual job.
  • Peer pressure to revert to old ways of working.
  • Training content not relevant to actual job tasks.
  • Too long a gap between training and opportunity to apply.
Strategies to Maximize Transfer
  • Manager briefing before training — set expectations and create application plan.
  • Post-training action plans — specific commitments on how/when new skills will be applied.
  • Structured follow-up coaching sessions 30/60 days post-training.
  • Peer learning groups and communities of practice for reinforcement.
  • Building new skills into performance goals — creating accountability for application.

G. Cultural Shock and Cross-Cultural Training

When employees are posted to a foreign country, they experience Cultural Shock — the disorientation and stress of encountering an unfamiliar culture.

Stages of Cultural Shock (HFAA Model)
  • H — Honeymoon Stage: Initial excitement and fascination with the new culture — everything seems interesting and exotic.
  • F — Frustration Stage: Differences become irritating; language barriers, different social norms, and bureaucracy create stress and homesickness.
  • A — Adjustment Stage: Gradual understanding and adaptation to the new culture — finding coping strategies and building local relationships.
  • A — Adaptation Stage: Comfortable functioning in the new culture while retaining home-culture identity. True cross-cultural competence achieved.
HR Interventions for Cross-Cultural Training
  • Pre-departure cultural awareness programs for the employee and family.
  • Language training in the host country's language.
  • Host-country mentor/buddy assignment for the first 3 months.
  • Virtual communities connecting expatriates across the same host country.
  • Repatriation planning — career path secured before departure to reduce return anxiety.

A. Performance Appraisal

Performance Appraisal is a systematic, periodic, and impartial evaluation of an employee's job performance and potential for development, relative to pre-established standards.

Nature and Objectives of Performance Appraisal
  • Systematic, not ad hoc — based on pre-defined criteria and standards.
  • Periodic — annual, semi-annual, or continuous (modern trend).
  • Basis for compensation decisions — increment, bonus, promotion.
  • Identifies training and development needs — where are the skill gaps?
  • Provides feedback — employees know where they stand and how to improve.
  • Supports HR planning — succession planning, promotion decisions, transfers.
  • Documents performance for disciplinary or termination decisions.
6-Step Performance Appraisal Process
  1. Establish Performance Standards: Define clear, measurable, job-relevant standards (KPIs, OKRs, SMART goals) — ideally set collaboratively in MBO.
  2. Communicate Standards: Ensure employees clearly understand what is expected — what "meets expectations" vs "exceeds expectations" looks like.
  3. Measure Actual Performance: Collect data throughout the year — not just at year-end. Sources: manager observations, self-assessment, peer feedback, project outcomes, client feedback.
  4. Compare Against Standards: Identify gaps between actual performance and required standards. Determine performance rating.
  5. Discuss Results (Appraisal Interview): One-on-one meeting between manager and employee to discuss rating, provide constructive feedback, and agree on development actions. Most critical and most often done poorly.
  6. Initiate Corrective Action: For under-performers — Performance Improvement Plan (PIP). For high-performers — stretch assignments, fast-track promotion, development opportunities.

  • Point Rating Method: Most systematic — each job factor given points.
  • Factor Comparison Method: Benchmark jobs used as reference.

HR Analytics is the application of data analysis and statistical techniques to HR data to make evidence-based people management decisions. It transforms HR from an intuition-based to a data-driven function.

LAMP provides the framework for impactful HR measurement. Each letter represents a critical element:

LetterStands forKey Question

AAnalyticsHow do we analyze the data? What statistical methods?
MMeasuresWhat data do we collect? How do we operationalize variables?
PProcessHow do we use these insights to drive decisions and change?
LAMP = Logic → Analytics → Measures → Process

Four Levels of HR Analytics (Maturity Levels)

  2. Diagnostic Analytics: "Why did it happen?" — Root cause analysis. Why is turnover high? Correlating engagement scores with attrition.

A 9-step framework for evidence-based HR decision making: Define problem → Collect data → Build model → Evaluate model → Deploy model → Critique → Refine.

Q1. What is Advertising? Explain the DAGMAR approach and steps in building an advertising programme.DAGMAR is a classic — appears almost every year
⌄
Advertising is any paid, non-personal form of communication about products, services, or ideas by an identified sponsor, through mass media. It is the most visible element of the promotional mix.

Functions/Importance of Advertising

  • Creates awareness about products and brands
  • Stimulates demand and increases sales
  • Builds brand image and brand equity
  • Supports personal selling efforts
  • Educates consumers about product features and uses
  • Counters competition and defends market share

DAGMAR Approach

DAGMAR = Defining Advertising Goals for Measured Advertising Results. Developed by Russell Colley (1961).

DAGMAR states that advertising goals must be specific, measurable communication tasks. Consumer moves through 4 stages:

StageDescriptionAdvertising Objective
UnawarenessConsumer unaware of productCreate awareness
AwarenessConsumer knows brand existsBuild comprehension
ComprehensionUnderstands product benefitsDrive conviction
ConvictionPositive attitude formedMotivate action/purchase
DAGMAR stages → U→A→C→C→A: Unawareness → Awareness → Comprehension → Conviction → Action

Steps in Building an Advertising Programme (5 M's)

  1. Mission (M1): What are the advertising objectives? Awareness, persuasion, reminder, or reinforcement?
  2. Money (M2): What is the advertising budget? Methods: Percentage of sales, competitive parity, objective-task method.
  3. Message (M3): What message to communicate? Copy development — content, structure, format, source. Appeals: rational, emotional, moral.
  4. Media (M4): Which media to use? Reach, frequency, impact considerations. TV, digital, print, outdoor, radio.
  5. Measurement (M5): How to evaluate the advertising's effectiveness? Pre-test and post-test methods.
Conclusion: DAGMAR provides a scientific, measurable approach to advertising goal-setting, moving away from vague objectives to specific communication milestones. The 5 M's ensure a comprehensive advertising programme covering all key decision areas.

Q2. What is Media Planning? Explain the factors affecting media selection and methods of setting the advertising budget.Highly expected — covers Unit II thoroughly
⌄
Media Planning is the process of selecting the most appropriate media channels to deliver the advertising message to the target audience effectively and efficiently.

Key Media Planning Terms

  • Reach: The number (or %) of target audience exposed to the message at least once.
  • Frequency: Average number of times the target audience is exposed to the message.
  • Impact: Qualitative value of exposure through a given medium.
  • GRP (Gross Rating Points): Reach × Frequency = Total weight of media schedule.
  • CPM (Cost per Thousand): Cost to reach 1,000 people. Used to compare media efficiency.

Factors Affecting Media Selection

  • Target Audience: Who are they? Where do they consume media? Demographics, psychographics.
  • Nature of Product: FMCG (TV/radio) vs B2B industrial (trade journals) vs tech (digital).
  • Advertising Budget: TV is expensive; digital is more affordable and targeted.
  • Message Characteristics: Complex messages need print; emotional messages suit TV/video.
  • Competitor's Media Choice: Staying visible in same media as competitors.
  • Geographic Coverage: National vs regional vs local campaign scope.
  • Media Availability: Some media have long booking lead times.

Methods of Setting Advertising Budget

  • Percentage of Sales Method: Fixed % of past or projected sales. Simple but circular logic.
  • Competitive Parity Method: Match or slightly exceed competitor's ad spend. Maintains share of voice.
  • Affordable Method: What the company can afford after other expenses. Ignores advertising's investment role.
  • Objective-Task Method: Best method. Define objectives → determine tasks to achieve them → calculate cost. Links budget directly to goals.
Conclusion: Effective media planning maximizes advertising ROI by ensuring the right message reaches the right audience through the right channel at the right time and cost. The objective-task method is most scientifically sound for budget setting.

Q4. What is Advertising Effectiveness? Explain pre-testing and post-testing methods.Measurement of advertising — highly asked
⌄
Advertising effectiveness measures the degree to which advertising achieves its stated communication and sales objectives. Measuring effectiveness is critical to justify ad budgets and optimize future campaigns.

Why Measure Advertising Effectiveness?

  • Justify advertising expenditure to management
  • Identify what works and what doesn't
  • Improve future campaigns
  • Optimize media mix and creative strategy

Pre-Testing Methods (Before release)

  • Consumer Jury: Panel of target consumers rate the ad.
  • Portfolio Test: Ad shown among other ads — recall tested later.
  • Theatre/Lab Test: Audience shown ad in controlled setting; attitude measured before and after.
  • Readability Test (Cloze Test): Measures comprehension of ad copy.
  • Eye Tracking: Technology tracks where consumers look on the ad — attention measurement.
  • Focus Group Testing: Qualitative discussion about ad perception with 8–10 consumers.

Post-Testing Methods (After release)

  • Recognition Test (Starch Test): Show ad to readers — measure % who saw, associated, read most.
  • Recall Test (Day-After Recall): 24 hours after TV ad, viewers called — unaided recall of brand/message.
  • Sales Effect Measurement: Compare sales before and after campaign. Most difficult — many variables affect sales.
  • Brand Awareness Tracking: Periodic surveys tracking aided/unaided brand awareness scores.
  • Digital Analytics: Click-through rate (CTR), impressions, conversion rate, cost per acquisition (CPA).
Conclusion: Pre-testing reduces the risk of expensive campaign failures; post-testing provides accountability and learning for future campaigns. Digital advertising has revolutionized effectiveness measurement with real-time, granular data.

Q4. What is Bancassurance? Explain its concept, models, benefits, and challenges.Bancassurance — unique to BBI students, often asked
⌄
Bancassurance is the distribution of insurance products (life and non-life) through a bank's distribution channels — branches, internet banking, mobile banking — to the bank's existing customer base.

Models of Bancassurance

  • Pure Distribution Model: Bank acts as an agent/broker — no ownership stake in insurance company. Simply sells insurance products and earns commission.
  • Exclusive Agreement Model: Bank has exclusive tie-up with one insurance company. Products marketed exclusively. (SBI + SBI Life Insurance)
  • Joint Venture Model: Bank and insurance company jointly set up a new insurance entity. Both share ownership, profits, and risk. (HDFC Bank + Standard Life → HDFC Life)
  • Fully Integrated Model: Bank and insurance functions fully integrated within one financial conglomerate — most complex. Common in Europe.

Benefits of Bancassurance

  • For Banks: Diversified revenue stream (commission income), deeper customer relationships, enhanced product portfolio, improved customer retention.
  • For Insurance Companies: Access to large, trusted customer base at lower distribution cost, extensive branch network, cross-selling opportunities.
  • For Customers: One-stop financial shop, trusted advisor (bank), simplified purchase process, bundled financial solutions.

Challenges of Bancassurance

Financial services, like all services, have four unique characteristics that distinguish them from physical products and create special marketing challenges:

IHIP Characteristics

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI), established on April 1, 1935 under the RBI Act 1934, is India's central bank. It regulates the monetary and financial system of India, maintaining price stability and financial stability.

  • Foreign Exchange Management: Manages India's foreign exchange reserves. Administers FEMA 1999.

  • Developmental Role: Promotes financial inclusion, priority sector lending norms.

Q2. Explain the key provisions of the Banking Regulation Act 1949. What regulatory powers does it give to RBI over banks?BR Act — second most important topic
⌄
The Banking Regulation Act 1949 (BR Act) is the primary legislation governing the business of banking in India. It empowers RBI to regulate, supervise, and control all banking companies, ensuring depositor protection and system stability.

Key Provisions of BR Act 1949

  • Definition of Banking Business (Section 5b): Accepting deposits from the public for lending and investment. Clearly defines what constitutes banking.
  • Licensing (Section 22): No bank can commence banking business without a license from RBI. RBI can cancel a license for non-compliance.
  • Capital Requirements (Section 11-12): Minimum paid-up capital and reserve requirements. Prohibits dividend payment from paid-up capital.
  • Cash Reserve Ratio — CRR (Section 42 of RBI Act, referenced in BR Act): Minimum % of deposits to be held as cash with RBI.
  • Statutory Liquidity Ratio — SLR (Section 24): Banks must maintain specified % of their liabilities in approved liquid assets (cash, gold, govt. securities). Currently 18%.
  • Restrictions on Non-Banking Business (Section 8): Banks cannot engage in non-banking activities (trading in goods) except for recovery of bad debts.
  • Board of Directors (Section 10-10B): Majority must be persons with professional experience. CEO appointment requires RBI approval.
  • Branch Licensing: Opening, closing, or shifting of branches requires RBI's prior approval.
  • Inspection Powers (Section 35): RBI can inspect any bank at any time; access all books, accounts, documents.
  • Reconstruction and Amalgamation (Section 44-45): RBI can arrange mergers of weak banks with stronger ones. Example: SBI absorbing associate banks.
  • Moratorium (Section 45): RBI can place a failing bank under moratorium — suspend operations and payments temporarily to facilitate rescue.

RBI's Regulatory Powers under BR Act

  • License granting and cancellation
  • Prescribe minimum capital and reserve requirements
  • Control management appointments (CEO, directors)
  • Inspect and audit banks without notice
  • Issue directions and guidelines on lending, interest rates, etc.
  • Impose penalties and corrective actions (Prompt Corrective Action — PCA framework)
  • Order merger, amalgamation, or winding up of banks
Conclusion: The BR Act gives RBI comprehensive powers to ensure banking system safety and soundness. Its provisions on licensing, capital requirements, and supervisory powers create a robust regulatory framework that protects depositors and maintains financial stability.

Q3. Explain the SARFAESI Act 2002 — objectives, key provisions, and role in NPA recovery.SARFAESI — guaranteed question every year
⌄
SARFAESI Act 2002 = Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act. It empowers banks to recover NPAs without court intervention through enforcement of security interest.

Three Key Pillars of SARFAESI

  1. Securitisation: Banks pool standard loans (mortgages, auto loans) and convert them into tradeable securities (Pass-Through Certificates — PTCs) sold to investors, freeing capital for fresh lending.
  2. Asset Reconstruction: Asset Reconstruction Companies (ARCs) — specialized institutions that buy NPAs from banks at a discount and try to recover maximum value. ARCs registered with RBI. Examples: ARCIL (Asset Reconstruction Company (India) Ltd), Phoenix ARC.
  3. Enforcement of Security Interest (Core Power): When loan becomes NPA (90+ days overdue), bank issues 60-day notice to borrower. If no payment, bank can: (a) Take possession of secured asset, (b) Sell asset through public auction/private treaty, (c) Take over management of borrower's business.

Applicability and Exclusions

  • Applies to: Secured loans above ₹1 lakh by banks, FIs, and ARCs.
  • Does NOT apply to: Agricultural land, education loans below ₹10 lakh, loans below ₹1 lakh, unsecured loans.

Borrower's Rights and Recourse

  • Borrower can respond to the 60-day notice with objections.
  • Can appeal to the Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT) within 45 days of bank's action.
  • DRT can stay the bank's action if sufficient cause shown.
  • Further appeal to Debt Recovery Appellate Tribunal (DRAT) and High Court.

Benefits to the Banking System

  • Faster NPA recovery without lengthy court proceedings (SARFAESI vs DRT vs civil courts — speed comparison)
  • Improves banks' NPA ratios and asset quality
  • Securitization frees up capital for fresh productive lending
  • ARCs create a secondary market for distressed assets
Conclusion: SARFAESI Act has significantly strengthened banks' NPA recovery arsenal. Combined with IBC 2016, DRTs, and Lok Adalats, India now has a comprehensive debt resolution ecosystem. Post-SARFAESI, India moved up in the World Bank's ease of doing business "resolving insolvency" rankings.

Q5. What is the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) 2016? Explain its process and significance for banking.IBC — newer legislation, increasingly tested
⌄
The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) 2016 is landmark legislation that consolidates all laws related to insolvency and bankruptcy in India for individuals, firms, and corporations. It provides a time-bound, market-driven resolution mechanism for stressed assets.

Key Bodies under IBC

  • IBBI (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India): Regulatory body overseeing IBC implementation, registration of IPs and IUs.
  • NCLT (National Company Law Tribunal): Adjudicating authority for corporate insolvency cases.
  • Resolution Professional (RP): Insolvency Professional who manages the corporate debtor during CIRP.
  • Committee of Creditors (CoC): Banks and financial creditors who vote on resolution plans.

Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP)

  1. Application: Financial/operational creditor or corporate debtor files application with NCLT. Minimum default: ₹1 crore.
  2. Admission (14 days): NCLT admits the application. Moratorium declared — no legal actions against company.
  3. Resolution Professional: Appointed by NCLT. Takes over management from existing directors.
  4. CIRP Period (180+90 days): Resolution plan invited from resolution applicants. CoC evaluates plans.
  5. Resolution or Liquidation: If CoC approves a plan (66% vote), company is rescued. If no plan accepted within deadline, company goes to liquidation — assets sold and proceeds distributed.

Significance for Banks (Creditors)

  • Banks get a seat at the table through the Committee of Creditors (CoC).
  • Faster resolution — 180+90 days vs years-long court battles earlier.
  • Maximizes asset value vs liquidation (going concern vs break-up value).
  • Resolution applications include cases like Bhushan Steel, Essar Steel — significant NPA recovery for banks.
  • Deterrent effect — borrowers less likely to default strategically knowing swift CIRP action possible.
Conclusion: IBC 2016 has transformed India's insolvency ecosystem. Its time-bound process, creditor control through CoC, and market-driven solutions have significantly improved NPA resolution outcomes, complementing SARFAESI Act for secured lending recovery.

Q6. What is NPA (Non-Performing Asset)? Explain its types and impact on banks.NPA — fundamental banking concept, short answer
⌄

NPA (Non-Performing Asset) is a loan or advance where the principal or interest payment is overdue for more than 90 days. Once classified as NPA, the bank must make provisions (set aside funds) for potential loss.

Types of NPA (Asset Classification)

  • Substandard Asset: NPA for less than or equal to 12 months. Some weakness but still some recovery possible.
  • Doubtful Asset: NPA for more than 12 months. Full recovery doubtful. Provision requirement: 25–100% depending on age.
  • Loss Asset: Considered uncollectible by auditors/RBI. Either written off or fully provided. Provision: 100%.

Impact of NPAs on Banks

  • Reduce profitability — high provisioning requirements eat into profits.
  • Reduce credit-giving capacity — capital locked in bad loans.
  • Increase cost of funds — risk premium demanded by depositors.
  • Damage bank's reputation and credit rating.
  • May trigger RBI's Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) — restrictions on lending and dividend payment.

Q7. What is the Banking Regulation Act's provision on SLR and CRR? Why are they required?SLR/CRR — basic banking law short answer
⌄

SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio) — Section 24 of BR Act: Banks must maintain a minimum % of their Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) in liquid assets (cash, gold, government securities). Current rate: 18%.

CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio) — Section 42 of RBI Act: Banks must keep a minimum % of their NDTL as cash with RBI (earns no interest). Current rate: 4%.

Why CRR and SLR are Required

  • CRR: Ensures banks always have liquid cash available; enables RBI to control money supply and credit creation in the economy.
  • SLR: Ensures bank solvency by maintaining liquid assets; also ensures a captive market for government securities; limits over-expansion of credit.
  • Combined effect: These pre-emptions (22% of deposits) create a buffer against bank runs and ensure systemic stability.
